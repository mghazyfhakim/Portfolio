     4→     4→  tasteskill v2, the anti-slop frontend skill. Art direction for websites, distilled from a study of

     8→     8→  this whenever you build, design, redesign, restyle, polish or review a landing page, marketing site,
     9→     9→  portfolio, product page, one-pager, hero or any public-facing web page, in any stack (HTML/CSS, React,
    10→    10→  Next.js, Vue, Svelte, Astro, Tailwind) — also when the user says a page looks generic, templated,
    11→    11→  "AI-made", sloppy, or asks to make it look designed, premium, award-level or less like every other
    12→    12→  site. Also for app screens: dashboards, admin and analytics views, settings, inboxes and other screens
    13→    13→  people work in, with their own run and rules.

    15→    15→
    16→    16→# tasteskill v2
    17→    17→
    18→    18→A page built by a model usually looks like the average of every page the model has seen: a centred
    19→    19→headline over three lines, a little label above it, a row of icon cards, a soft gradient, a few thin
    20→    20→rules and a monospace caption. Nothing in it is wrong and nothing in it was decided.
    21→    21→
    22→    22→This skill replaces those defaults with decisions, in four ways:
    23→    23→
    24→    24→1. **A script deals the directions, you choose among them.** Asked to invent a look, a model picks
    25→    25→   the category default every time. `scripts/draw.mjs` deals three of twenty researched directions;
    26→    26→   you choose the one that carries the subject's idea, and the script then deals its type system,
    27→    27→   palette, hero and section compositions from a library distilled from 247 real sites.
    28→    28→2. **The idea comes from the subject.** In the study, what separated the best sites from the forgettable
    29→    29→   ones was a concept taken from the thing itself, not the styling.
    30→    30→3. **A house style with no ornaments,** measured in a real browser by `scripts/inspect.mjs`, so the
    31→    31→   rules are checked rather than hoped for.
    32→    32→4. **You look at what you made.** Screenshots at three widths, a juror's rubric, fix, look again.
    33→    33→
    34→    34→One warning learned in testing. Taking the ornaments away is only half the job; a page that has lost its
    35→    35→labels, rules and cards and gained nothing in their place reads as thin, and blind jurors rightly prefer
    36→    36→a richer page with tells over an empty page without them. The other half is substance: type set
    37→    37→larger, whole fields of colour, real pictures of this subject's world (found or drawn), and every
    38→    38→section carrying its full content. The skill is built to give you that; do not trade it for restraint.
    39→    39→
    40→    40→In the commands below `<skill>` is this skill's folder. Run them from the project's root, so the
    41→    41→working files land in `.taste-skill/` there. The scripts need Node 22+ and an installed Chrome, Edge
    42→    42→or Chromium, and install nothing. `assets.mjs` also needs network access; it uses no API keys.
    43→    43→
    44→    44→## Page or app screen
    45→    45→
    46→    46→Decide first which of two kinds of work this is.
    47→    47→
    48→    48→- **A page** is read and scrolled: a landing page, a marketing or product site, a portfolio, an
    49→    49→  event, a restaurant, a one-pager. It follows **the run** below.
    50→    50→- **An app screen** is worked in: a dashboard, an admin or analytics view, settings, billing, an inbox
    51→    51→  or queue to work through, a wallet, a list with filters, an editor or builder. It follows
    52→    52→  **`references/app-screens.md`**: its own run, its own draw (`draw.mjs --app`), its own house rules
    53→    53→  (quiet borders, small labels and repeated panels are normal there; gradients, glows, icons in tinted
    54→    54→  squares, rainbow charts and invented vanity numbers are not) and `inspect.mjs --app`. Read that file
    55→    55→  whole before you start; the page run below does not apply.
    56→    56→
    57→    57→A product site that shows its app is a page; the interface fragments on it follow the app-screen
    58→    58→rules for their insides. Deal the product's app style once, in its own folder, with the page's colours
    59→    59→and type kept:
    60→    60→
    61→    61→```bash
    62→    62→node <skill>/scripts/draw.mjs --app --kind <kind> --brief "<the product>" --keep palette,type --out .taste-skill/app
    63→    63→```
    64→    64→
    65→    65→and build every fragment the page shows (the hero's live screen, a feature's crop, a chart) as a real
    66→    66→piece of that app: `assets/app.css` for its panels, tables and controls, `assets/charts.js` for its
    67→    67→charts, real data that adds up, one finding stated, cropped and scaled the way the page's blueprint
    68→    68→treats a picture. A fragment that looks like a working product is the strongest picture a software page
    69→    69→has; grey bars and lorem numbers are the weakest. When a request mixes both (a landing page and the
    70→    70→dashboard behind it), build each with its own run and one shared palette and typeface.
    71→    71→
    72→    72→## The run
    73→    73→
    74→    74→Do these in order. Steps 1–5 take a few minutes and decide whether the page is any good.
    75→    75→
    76→    76→### 1. Read the brief and the project
    77→    77→
    78→    78→Find out what is real: the subject, the audience, the one thing the page must get someone to do, the
    79→    79→stack, any existing brand (colours, fonts, logo), and above all **what imagery truly exists**: the
    80→    80→project's own photographs or product shots, a real interface that can be shown, an image generation
    81→    81→tool you can call, or nothing. Do not plan around pictures you do not have. Nothing of its own is
    82→    82→rarely nothing at all: the subject's city, street, materials and history are in open archives and
    83→    83→museums, free to use with a credit (step 6, `scripts/assets.mjs`).
    84→    84→
    85→    85→If the request is vague, decide and state your reading in one line instead of asking. Ask only when a
    86→    86→missing fact blocks the work (for example, no product name).
    87→    87→
    88→    88→Then **inventory the subject** in your notes: eight to twelve things that belong to this client and to
    89→    89→no competitor. Look for its name and what the name means; its address, door number, street and
    90→    90→neighbourhood, and what that place looks like (its tiles, signs, paint colours, typefaces on its shop
    91→    91→fronts, its light, its weather); the numbers in the brief (seats, rooms, minutes to the sea, the
    92→    92→price, the year); the constraint it lives with (only one batch a month, closed in winter, by
    93→    93→appointment); its materials and colours; its rituals and hours (the oven lit at five, the ferry at
    94→    94→seven); the printed things of its world (a timetable, a label, a ticket, a sign). This list is where
    95→    95→the idea comes from in step 4. In blind tests, the page that won each brief was built from an item on
    96→    96→such a list (the client's name and what it means, the street it stands in, the one constraint that
    97→    97→makes it what it is), and the pages that lost had a good composition with a category idea. The list
    98→    98→is also your picture search: each place, material and printed thing on it is a query for step 6.
    99→    99→
   100→   100→### 2. Write the argument
   101→   101→
   102→   102→Before any design, list the page as claims with their proof, one line per section, the way
   103→   103→`references/copy.md` §1 shows. The claims decide which sections exist and in what order. Delete any
   104→   104→section that has no claim. This is how you avoid the stock sequence of hero, logos, three features,
   105→   105→testimonial, pricing, call to action.
   106→   106→
   107→   107→### 3. Draw the direction
   108→   108→
   109→   109→```bash
   110→   110→node <skill>/scripts/draw.mjs --sector <sector> --brief "<one line about the subject>" \
   111→   111→  --sections <roles in the order of your argument> [--tone <tone>] [--have <assets>] [--keep palette,type] \
   112→   112→  --options 3
   113→   113→```
   114→   114→
   115→   115→This deals three directions and prints a short summary of each. Hold each one against your inventory:
   116→   116→which way of composing can carry something only this client has? Choose that one, not the most
   117→   117→familiar, and write one line in your notes on why. In testing, a single random direction that did not
   118→   118→suit the subject was the commonest cause of a weak page. Then deal the full sheet:
   119→   119→
   120→   120→```bash
   121→   121→node <skill>/scripts/draw.mjs <the same arguments, without --options> --direction <chosen id>
   122→   122→```
   123→   123→
   124→   124→- `--sector`: `node <skill>/scripts/draw.mjs --list sectors`. Pick the closest.
   125→   125→- `--sections`: roles from `--list roles` (features, showcase, how-it-works, testimonial, proof, stats,
   126→   126→  pricing, faq, cta, footer, intro, about, work, gallery, list, contact). The hero is implied.
   127→   127→- `--tone` (optional): quiet, bold, warm, technical, playful, luxury, editorial. Only pass it when the
   128→   128→  brief clearly asks for one.
   129→   129→- `--have`: assets that really exist, comma-separated: `photography` (the project's own photographs of
   130→   130→  the place, people or work), `product-photo` (photographs of the specific product, or an image tool
   131→   131→  that can make them), `product-ui` (real screenshots), `illustration`, `video`. Leave it out if you
   132→   132→  have none: the draw then avoids compositions that depend on them and the sheet says how to fill
   133→   133→  picture slots honestly (software shows its own interface built in HTML; a product with no photographs
   134→   134→  is drawn and set among real pictures of its world, never a fake product shot). Pictures you will
   135→   135→  find in the open collections are not the project's own; leave them out of `--have`.
   136→   136→- `--keep palette,type`: when the brand already fixes colours or typefaces. The draw then deals only
   137→   137→  the composition, and you map the brand's values onto the roles.
   138→   138→
   139→   139→The command prints a **direction sheet**. It is the art direction for this build:
   140→   140→
   141→   141→- **Adopt it.** Do not blend it with the layout you would have made anyway. If it feels unfamiliar,
   142→   142→  good: the familiar layout is the one this skill exists to prevent.
   143→   143→- Redraw one slot (`--redraw hero | type | palette | photos | scenes | section:<role> | direction`) only for a
   144→   144→  reason you can state: it contradicts a stated brand constraint, or needs an asset that does not
   145→   145→  exist. Never redraw to get something more comfortable. Record every redraw in your report.
   146→   146→- When a dealt section cannot hold the real content (it compares three plans and there is one price;
   147→   147→  it wants five quotations and there are none), do not bend the content to fit. List the others with
   148→   148→  `--alternatives <role>`, pick the one whose assumptions match the content, and take it with
   149→   149→  `--redraw section:<role>=<id>`.
   150→   150→- **Check the palette against the subject.** A dealt palette is a set of proportions (one ground, one
   151→   151→  ink, one accent, fields) as much as a set of colours. If its colours fight the subject's own material
   152→   152→  (candy pastels for a peat whisky, neon for a mountain hut), redraw the palette once; if the subject
   153→   153→  has unmistakable colours of its own (its material, the paint of its street, the landscape outside
   154→   154→  its windows), keep the dealt proportions and take the colours from the subject instead, and say so
   155→   155→  in the report.
   156→   156→- Reprint it any time with `node <skill>/scripts/draw.mjs --show`. In a long session, re-read it before each
   157→   157→  section you build.
   158→   158→
   159→   159→### 4. Find the idea
   160→   160→
   161→   161→The direction is a way of composing. The idea is what makes the page belong to this subject and no
   162→   162→other. Write this sentence in your notes and do not start building until both blanks are filled from
   163→   163→the subject itself:
   164→   164→
   165→   165→> On this page, **[something that belongs to the subject]** becomes **[the device the direction calls for]**.
   166→   166→
   167→   167→Places to look for the first blank:
   168→   168→
   169→   169→- **Its output.** What does the thing produce? Put the real output on the page as material. (A camera
   170→   170→  that prints poems delivered its copy as paper slips.)
   171→   171→- **Its object or shape.** What does it physically look like, in cross-section, in silhouette?
   172→   172→  Enlarge it, crop it, make it the frame or the one motif.
   173→   173→- **Its name.** Can the letters hold a photograph, stack into a wall, split to two corners?
   174→   174→- **Its place.** Is there one view that makes someone want to be there?
   175→   175→- **Its ritual.** Is there a sequence the page can perform as it scrolls?
   176→   176→- **Its artefact.** What printed thing already exists in its world: ticket, menu, label, receipt,
   177→   177→  invitation, folder, manual?
   178→   178→- **Its opposite.** Which other category owns the feeling this one lacks?
   179→   179→
   180→   180→Fill the first blank from your inventory (step 1), not from the category. **The competitor test:** put
   181→   181→the nearest real competitor in the sentence (the bakery two streets away, another oak furniture maker,
   182→   182→another hut on the same mountain). If the sentence still works, the idea belongs to the category: a
   183→   183→loaf of bread is every bakery; the seven doors of the street this one is named after are only this one.
   184→   184→"Clean and modern" is not an idea. The sites the study's panel scored lowest were the ones whose idea
   185→   185→was recorded as "none beyond the standard formula".
   186→   186→
   187→   187→**One live thing.** Give the page one thing the visitor can do, or that is different for each visit,
   188→   188→made from the idea: pick a wood and see the chair in it; type your name and see it cut in the
   189→   189→foundry's letters; set the loan and read the real monthly figure; see whether the hut's stove is lit
   190→   190→right now and how long until the last ferry; choose a size and see it against the room. In blind tests every winning page had one and the jurors named it first. Rules: it uses the subject's real content, it works on a phone with a thumb, it has a plain
   191→   191→fallback without script, it never blocks reading, and it is one thing, not a playground. It is
   192→   192→built in step 6 as part of the page, not added as motion in step 9.
   193→   193→
   194→   194→**The scenes carry the idea too.** The scroll scenes on the sheet are devices waiting for a subject.
   195→   195→Decide what each one shows from your inventory before building: the fly-through passes through this
   196→   196→studio's own title cards, the route is the road this shipment really takes, the picture that opens to
   197→   197→the whole screen is the room the guest will wake up in. A scene filled with pictures that could belong
   198→   198→to anyone is a screensaver.
   199→   199→
   200→   200→### 5. Write the words
   201→   201→
   202→   202→Write the whole page as a copy deck (plain text) before markup, following `references/copy.md`:
   203→   203→
   204→   204→- **Every headline fits in two lines or fewer at every width.** Do the arithmetic in copy.md §2 before
   205→   205→  committing to a sentence: about 32 characters is safe everywhere at a confident size. The studied
   206→   206→  gallery heroes have a median of 5 words and 32 characters.
   207→   207→- One supporting sentence, directly under the headline (32–48px below it) and shown with it, never
   208→   208→  docked at the foot of the first screen or held back by an animation. When the headline is a name or
   209→   209→  a figure of speech, this sentence says plainly what the thing is. One primary action, named for what
   210→   210→  it does, as a real button on the first screen.
   211→   211→- **The first screen answers the visitor's first question in large type.** For an event: what it is,
   212→   212→  when and where (the dates belong in the headline or at the second size, never only in small text).
   213→   213→  For a product: what it is and what it costs or does. For a person: who and what they do. Every
   214→   214→  section the visitor came for (the line-up, the menu, the rooms, the price) has its own heading and
   215→   215→  its own place in the navigation.
   216→   216→- **Write the full content.** Every section the brief asks for delivers its information completely:
   217→   217→  the whole menu with prices, each room with its own details, every speaker and talk, the price with
   218→   218→  what it includes, an answer for each real question. Thin sections are the commonest reason a clean
   219→   219→  page loses to a busier one.
   220→   220→- Sample details are expected where the brief leaves gaps (dish names, room names and rates, project
   221→   221→  titles) so the design is seen at full density; list them in the report for the client to confirm.
   222→   222→  Proof is never invented: no customers, quotes, reviews, press, logos or business statistics.
   223→   223→
   224→   224→### 6. Build it, still
   225→   225→
   226→   226→Build the page with motion switched off in your head; a page that needs animation to look finished is
   227→   227→not finished. Order of work:
   228→   228→
   229→   229→1. **Tokens.** Colour and type roles from the sheet as CSS custom properties. Everything downstream
   230→   230→   references tokens only. `<skill>/assets/base.css` is a neutral foundation you may start from (reset, token
   231→   231→   names, fluid type steps, grid, focus and selection styles, the motion helpers); it has no look of
   232→   232→   its own.
   233→   233→2. **The hero, exactly as the blueprint describes it,** at desktop and at phone width. Get the big
   234→   234→   thing big and the small things small before anything else exists.
   235→   235→3. **Each section from its blueprint,** with this project's content. Keep the geometry, change the
   236→   236→   subject. Neighbouring sections must not share a layout. **Carry the idea into every section,**
   237→   237→   pricing, FAQ and footer included: the commonest failure in the study, even among good sites, is a
   238→   238→   distinctive first screen followed by stock blocks.
   239→   239→4. **Imagery,** in this order of preference:
   240→   240→   1. the project's own;
   241→   241→   2. generated, if you have an image tool;
   242→   242→   3. **real pictures of the subject's world from the open collections,** whenever an honest one
   243→   243→      exists. Search them with `node <skill>/scripts/assets.mjs "<query>"` (Openverse, Wikimedia
   244→   244→      Commons, the Art Institute of Chicago and the Met; only licences that allow commercial use, by
   245→   245→      default). Search the inventory, not the category: its street in old postcards, an old map of the
   246→   246→      neighbourhood, its material up close, its trade in engravings, a museum object of its kind, a
   247→   247→      botanical plate, a star chart. Use them large and with intent: one archival photograph as the
   248→   248→      hero, an engraving enlarged into the page's motif, the real map of the street as the contact
   249→   249→      section. Look at each before choosing; `--get <ref>` downloads it into `assets/` and records
   250→   250→      its source and licence, and `--credits` prints the credit line for the footer. A found picture
   251→   251→      shows the world around the client and is never presented as the client's own product, room,
   252→   252→      dish, person or team;
   253→   253→   4. **drawn in code from the subject itself,** for what no honest picture exists of (its objects,
   254→   254→      its tiles, its windows, its product's interface, made as one consistent flat graphic language in
   255→   255→      the palette's real colours);
   256→   256→   5. the photographs dealt on the sheet, only where an honestly generic picture fits (the sea for a
   257→   257→      sea house), never as a stand-in for the subject;
   258→   258→   6. type and colour alone.
   259→   259→
   260→   260→   When the project has footage or a real picture of its own process or product, the first screen
   261→   261→   shows it, large (about 40% of the screen or more). Never write a sentence on the page about where
   262→   262→   the pictures come from or what they are not ("none of these shows our bar"): a caption says what
   263→   263→   the picture is, and the credit line sits small in the footer. A portfolio whose work is invented
   264→   264→   for the brief draws each piece as a finished still of that work (its title card with its own type,
   265→   265→   an end frame, a poster), with real detail, never as a pictogram of the client's product.
   266→   266→
   267→   267→   **The floor, which the inspector checks:** at least four real pictures on the page, usually six to
   268→   268→   twelve (photographs, archival images, maps, engravings, footage, raster artwork; for software, its
   269→   269→   cropped interface fragments count too). SVG drawings do not count toward the floor: draw what no
   270→   270→   picture exists of, and still carry real pictures. Add one moving picture wherever an honest clip
   271→   271→   exists: search it with `assets.mjs --video "<query>"` and use it as a muted loop with a poster
   272→   272→   frame. Models skip pictures and footage because finding them is work; the page fails without them.
   273→   273→
   274→   274→   `references/imagery.md` covers searching, licences and credits, video, drawing, crops and treatments.
   275→   275→5. **The live thing** from step 4, built into the section where it proves the claim (often the hero
   276→   276→   or the booking), with real content, a thumb-sized phone version and a plain fallback.
   277→   277→6. **The scroll scenes,** when the sheet deals them (section 9), each with the section it carries: the page's big
   278→   278→   moments, the camera that flies through the work, the picture that opens to the whole screen, the
   279→   279→   word that buckles under the scroll. Copy the shared stage and the scene's files from
   280→   280→   `<skill>/assets/scenes/`, fill the scene with this project's own pictures and words, and set the
   281→   281→   page's tokens so it takes the page's look. A scene is composition: it decides how long the page is
   282→   282→   and what each screen shows, so it is built now and seen in every look round, not added at the end.
   283→   283→   `references/scenes.md` says how to put one on a page, fill it, fit it and check it;
   284→   284→   `assets/scenes/demo/<id>.html` is a working example of each.
   285→   285→7. **Fullness.** Look at each desktop screen as a poster: one dominant thing and the content it
   286→   286→   carries. A screen that is mostly empty ground is an underbuilt section, not a rest; one deliberate
   287→   287→   rest per page is the limit, and the inspector fails a page with two or more near-empty screens.
   288→   288→   Running text is in the full ink at 16px or more; grey is for secondary notes.
   289→   289→8. **The frame.** The page has one pair of equal outer margins at every width, and every section uses
   290→   290→   the width the page has set up: when its text sits in the left half, the right half holds its
   291→   291→   picture, its second column, its list or its figure, never nothing. Asymmetry lives inside the grid,
   292→   292→   never in the frame: a void is placed only when something closes it on the far side. No stretch of
   293→   293→   the page taller than about half a screen holds nothing at all; sections are 120–240px apart on
   294→   294→   desktop. Every section the visitor came for opens with a heading that names it, and the first
   295→   295→   screen carries the page's title in an `h1`. These are what readers name first when a page looks
   296→   296→   wrong ("no clear title", "random empty space", "everything is pushed to one side"), and the
   297→   297→   inspector measures each of them.
   298→   298→9. **States and semantics.** Real links and buttons, visible focus, alt text, a phone layout that is
   299→   299→   its own composition.
   300→   300→
   301→   301→Read `references/type.md`, `references/colour.md` and `references/layout.md` when you need the detail
   302→   302→behind a decision; the laws below are the short form.
   303→   303→
   304→   304→### 7. Gate
   305→   305→
   306→   306→```bash
   307→   307→node <skill>/scripts/lint.mjs <source files or folder>
   308→   308→node <skill>/scripts/inspect.mjs <url or path/to/index.html>
   309→   309→node <skill>/scripts/scenes.mjs <url or path/to/index.html>     # when the page has scroll scenes
   310→   310→```
   311→   311→
   312→   312→`scenes.mjs` photographs every scroll scene at 0, 10 … 100% of its hold, on desktop and on a phone,
   313→   313→and the reduced-motion page; with scenes on the sheet, `inspect.mjs` also films the whole scroll
   314→   314→(`desktop-film-NN.jpg`). A scene that never started or rests on desktop is a `FAIL`.
   315→   315→
   316→   316→`lint.mjs` scans source for banned patterns and stock phrases. `inspect.mjs` opens the page in a real
   317→   317→browser at 1440, 768 and 390 pixels, measures it (headline line counts, type sizes, fonts actually
   318→   318→loaded, colours, rules, shadows, gradients, contrast, overflow, and the frame: equal margins, sections
   319→   319→huddled on one side, blank stretches, the title, untitled sections, text against the screen edge) and
   320→   320→saves screenshots to `.taste-skill/inspect/`. Fix every `FAIL`. Treat each `WARN` as wrong unless you can defend it to a
   321→   321→designer in one sentence. Re-run until there are no `FAIL`s. Do not argue with the meter and do not
   322→   322→hide things from it. The inspector serves local files over a throwaway local server; if the page must
   323→   323→also open straight from the file system, open `index.html` that way once before you finish.
   324→   324→
   325→   325→### 8. Look
   326→   326→
   327→   327→Passing the gate means nothing banned was measured. It does not mean the page is good. Open the
   328→   328→screenshots the inspector lists (every viewport, with your image viewer or Read tool) and critique them
   329→   329→with `references/critique.md`: the fourteen passes, the Awwwards-weighted score, the caps. Write the critique
   330→   330→down in its format, fix the top three problems, run the inspector again, look again. Three rounds at
   331→   331→most.
   332→   332→
   333→   333→Judge only the pictures. If a problem is visible in a screenshot, it is real, whatever the code says.
   334→   334→
   335→   335→### 9. Move
   336→   336→
   337→   337→Now build the motion layer. It is required, and it is more than one fade: the inspector counts the
   338→   338→elements that really move while the page loads and is scrolled, and fails a page under eight (it warns
   339→   339→under sixteen). Any scroll scenes are already built (step 6); around them the layer has four parts:
   340→   340→
   341→   341→1. **The signature move** named on the sheet: one, done well (a dealt scene can be it).
   342→   342→2. **An entrance for the parts of every section:** the heading, then the text, then the picture,
   343→   343→   staggered by 60–90ms, rising 16–32px with opacity, triggered once as the section enters. Not one
   344→   344→   fade for the whole block, and never content that stays hidden if the script fails.
   345→   345→3. **Feedback on every control:** press, hover and focus on buttons, links, cards and the live thing.
   346→   346→4. **One moving picture:** a found clip as a muted loop, a slow scale or drift on a photograph, or a
   347→   347→   scroll-linked move on the hero picture.
   348→   348→
   349→   349→Transform and opacity only; every move has a `prefers-reduced-motion` fallback that keeps the content
   350→   350→and drops the travel. A move that changes the colour or the layout of whole screens (colour by
   351→   351→chapter, a pinned story) is part of the composition, so build it in step 6 and let every look round see
   352→   352→it. `references/motion.md` has the tokens and tested recipes. Then run the inspector once more and check
   353→   353→every screenshot for content that failed to appear.
   354→   354→
   355→   355→### 10. Report
   356→   356→
   357→   357→Do not report until all of these are true; each one was skipped in a test run that came out badly:
   358→   358→
   359→   359→- `inspect.mjs` shows **0 FAIL** at 1440, 768 and 390, on the final version of the page.
   360→   360→- You opened every screenshot it listed after the last change, not only the first screen.
   361→   361→- With scroll scenes: `scenes.mjs` ran on the final page with no problems, and you read every sheet
   362→   362→  (desktop, phone, reduced motion).
   363→   363→- A stranger reading the first screen for five seconds can say what this is and what to do.
   364→   364→- Every section has a heading; the margins match on both sides; no stretch of the page is empty.
   365→   365→- The critique of your last round is written down in the shape of `references/critique.md` §4.
   366→   366→
   367→   367→Tell the user, briefly: the direction dealt (name and seed) and any redraws with reasons; the idea
   368→   368→sentence and the live thing; the final scores from your last critique round and what still falls short;
   369→   369→every found picture and clip with its title, creator, licence and source page (`assets.mjs --credits` prints
   370→   370→the list) and where its credit sits on the page; which bundled photographs and which drawings stand in
   371→   371→for photography (so they can be replaced); and any sample content that must be swapped before launch.
   372→   372→Do not claim a check you did not run.

   374→   374→## House rules
   375→   375→
   376→   376→These hold on every build, whatever the direction. `inspect.mjs` measures them.
   377→   377→
   378→   378→| Rule | Instead |
   379→   379→| --- | --- |
   380→   380→| **Headlines: two lines or fewer, at every width.** Every `h1`–`h6`, and the main line of the first screen whatever its tag. | Cut words. Then widen the measure. Size last. |
   381→   381→| **A clear title on the first screen**, in the page's one `h1`, and a heading on every section. | Say what this is: the name with what it does, the product with what it is for, the event with its date and place. |
   382→   382→| **Equal outer margins; no section huddled on one side** with the other half of the screen empty. | Close every void with something on its far side: the section's picture, a second column, a list, a figure. |
   383→   383→| **No blank stretches** taller than about half a screen. | The normal section gap. A deliberate rest is a placed picture or a field of colour, once per page. |
   384→   384→| **Statements are paragraphs.** An intro sentence or a quotation set large is marked up as `<p>` or `<blockquote>`, stays at statement size (36px or less on desktop, 24px on a phone) and runs to about 25 words, three lines. | Larger than that it is a headline and the two-line rule applies. In blind tests jurors read a 42px sentence over four lines as a four-line headline. |
   385→   385→| **No eyebrows.** No small label above a heading, no pill badge above the headline, no "New ·" chip. | Put the information in the heading or the first sentence, or drop it. |
   386→   386→| **No monospace** outside real code blocks. | The text face. Tabular figures where numbers align. |
   387→   387→| **No index numbering.** No 01 / 02 / 03, no (001), no "Step 2". | Order shown by position, size, or the words. A real `<ol>` if order matters. |
   388→   388→| **No thin rules.** No hairline dividers, no 1px outlined boxes, no grid lines, crosshairs or crop marks. | Space. A step in background tone. A change of scale. A filled shape. |
   389→   389→| **No gradients.** Not as backgrounds, not on buttons, not in text. | Flat colour, or a real photograph. A neutral scrim under text on a photo is the one exception. |
   390→   390→| **No shadows on things at rest.** No glows. | A tone step between surfaces. Shadows only on what truly floats: a menu, a dialog. |
   391→   391→| **No assistant palette.** No yellow-cream ground with terracotta or rust; no violet or indigo. | The dealt palette. |
   392→   392→| **No tiny uppercase letter-spaced labels.** | Sentence case at a readable size. |
   393→   393→| **No icon-topped feature cards, no rows of identical cards.** | The dealt section blueprint. |
   394→   394→| **No glass.** No backdrop blur panels, no floating pill navigation. | A plain bar: wordmark, a few links, one action. |
   395→   395→| **No emoji, no gradient blobs, no decorative shapes, no fake dashboards of grey bars.** | A real picture, a real interface fragment, or nothing. |
   396→   396→| **No styled accent word** in a headline (italic, serif, coloured, underlined). | One face, one colour. The words carry the emphasis. |
   397→   397→| **Two typefaces at most,** usually one. | The dealt type system. |
   398→   398→
   399→   399→Three places keep their own conventions and are left alone by the inspector: real code in
   400→   400→`<pre>`/`<code>` (monospace is correct there), real tables and form fields (they may have lines), and
   401→   401→a real interface fragment whose root carries the attribute `data-ui` (the product's own borders and
   402→   402→menus; see `references/imagery.md` §7).
   403→   403→
   404→   404→Why these, honestly. Some are what the study measured: among the 247 sites, those the panel scored
   405→   405→7.5 or higher used icon feature cards 1% of the time against 28% for sites under 6.5, pill badges 14%
   406→   406→against 34%, gradients 22% against 52%, shadows 24% against 48%, tiny tracked caps 16% against 57%.
   407→   407→Others are the owner's taste and go against current gallery fashion: monospace labels, index numbers
   408→   408→and hairline grids are actually *more* common on award sites than on weak ones, which is exactly why
   409→   409→generators imitate them and why they now read as costume. They are banned here without exception.
   410→   410→`references/tells.md` keeps the dated registry.
   411→   411→
   412→   412→Removing ornament leaves space. Never fill it with another ornament. Fill it with scale (make the main
   413→   413→thing larger), with the full content, with a picture made for this subject, or with a field of flat
   414→   414→colour. Leave it empty on purpose once per page at most.
   415→   415→
   416→   416→## Laws of the craft
   417→   417→
   418→   418→Short form. Numbers are from the study unless marked as house style.
   419→   419→
   420→   420→**Type**
   421→   421→- Two or three sizes do the visible work: one very large, one small, nothing in between. Median
   422→   422→  headline-to-body ratio on the studied sites is 4.3; the best go far beyond (largest type: median
   423→   423→  8.4vw on the top sites against 5.0vw on the weak ones).
   424→   424→- Display type: tracking -0.02 to -0.05em, leading 0.85–1.05. Text: 16–20px, leading 1.45–1.6,
   425→   425→  45–75 characters per line.
   426→   426→- Five sizes on a page is the ceiling. One family is normal (a third of the studied sites); two is the
   427→   427→  limit.
   428→   428→- Left-aligned by default. Centre at most one moment.
   429→   429→
   430→   430→**Space**
   431→   431→- One grid, one pair of tight margins (16–32px), used everywhere; large things run to the edges. Text
   432→   432→  blocks share two or three left edges on the whole page.
   433→   433→- Asymmetry inside the grid, never in the frame: the two outer margins are equal, and an off-centre
   434→   434→  heading is answered by something at the far side of the grid.
   435→   435→- The gap between sections is clearly larger than any gap inside one: 120–240px on desktop (the study's median is about 240).
   436→   436→- Each section has a different composition from its neighbours; none is a card grid.
   437→   437→
   438→   438→**Colour**
   439→   439→- One ground, one ink, one accent. The accent appears on the action and perhaps one other thing.
   440→   440→- Running text in the full ink. A page set in soft grey reads unfinished.
   441→   441→- Surfaces are separated by a small step in tone, never by a line or a shadow.
   442→   442→- A section may change the whole field colour. Nothing fades into anything.
   443→   443→
   444→   444→**Imagery**
   445→   445→- One dominant image beats six small ones. On the top-scored sites the hero media covers at least half
   446→   446→  the first screen 68% of the time.
   447→   447→- Same crop ratios, same treatment, square corners unless the direction says otherwise (73% of the
   448→   448→  studied sites leave media square).
   449→   449→- No image is better than a placeholder, an icon, or an abstract shape. A picture drawn for this
   450→   450→  subject, in its colours and with care, is better than either. A real picture of its world, found
   451→   451→  and credited, is better still.
   452→   452→- Every found picture is downloaded into the project, credited on the page and listed in the report.
   453→   453→  None is presented as the client's own.
   454→   454→- Nothing ever lies on top of a headline or a statement (the inspector fails it).
   455→   455→
   456→   456→**Components**
   457→   457→- Buttons: flat, one fill colour, either square-cornered or fully round, decided once (top sites: 52%
   458→   458→  square, 23% pill; the 9–16px "soft" radius is the weak-site default). Most strong heroes have no
   459→   459→  button at all, or one.
   460→   460→- Navigation: wordmark, three to five plain links, one action. No bar chrome.
   461→   461→- The footer is a designed close, not a sitemap: on most strong sites the name returns at full width.
   462→   462→
   463→   463→## Special cases
   464→   464→
   465→   465→**An existing brand or design system.** Pass `--keep palette,type`, map the brand's colours and faces
   466→   466→onto the roles, and take only composition from the draw. The house rules still apply; if the brand
   467→   467→itself requires a banned device (its logo sits in a gradient, its accent is violet), keep the brand
   468→   468→asset, say so in the report, and pass `--allow <rule>` to the inspector for that rule only.
   469→   469→
   470→   470→**Redesign, restyle or review of an existing page or app screen.**
   471→   471→
   472→   472→1. **Record the before.** Run the project as it is and inspect it into its own folder
   473→   473→   (`inspect.mjs <url> --out .taste-skill/before`, with `--app` for an app screen) and lint its
   474→   474→   source. Read the code: the framework, the styling method (Tailwind v3 or v4, CSS modules,
   475→   475→   styled-components, plain CSS), the components, and what must survive: routes, forms, data,
   476→   476→   behaviour, the brand's own assets.
   477→   477→2. **A review stops here.** Critique the before screenshots with `references/critique.md`, list the
   478→   478→   findings with the inspector's measurements, and name the three changes that would move the page
   479→   479→   most.
   480→   480→3. **Keep the stack, the content and what works.** No framework or styling-library migration; check the
   481→   481→   dependency file before adding anything. A restyle keeps the layout and takes type, colour and
   482→   482→   components from a dealt direction (`--keep` whatever the brand fixes); a redesign deals a full
   483→   483→   direction and rebuilds the sections from its blueprints inside the existing components. Either way
   484→   484→   proceed from step 2 of the run with the page's real content.
   485→   485→4. **Order of work, biggest effect for least risk:** type (faces and scale), colour (one ground, one
   486→   486→   ink, one accent), the frame (margins, section rhythm, a title on the first screen and a heading on
   487→   487→   every section), states (hover, focus, loading, empty, error), then the stock components (icon
   488→   488→   cards, pill badges, gradient blobs, card grids) replaced by the dealt blueprints.
   489→   489→5. **Prove it.** Inspect again into `.taste-skill/after`, compare before and after at every width,
   490→   490→   click through every link, form and menu that worked before, and report the before and after
   491→   491→   measurements side by side.
   492→   492→
   493→   493→**A component or a single section, not a page.** Skip the draw unless there is no direction to follow.
   494→   494→Match the surrounding design, apply the house rules and the laws, and still inspect the result.
   495→   495→
   496→   496→**No checkout, no form backend.** The primary button goes to the real destination when the brief names
   497→   497→one. Otherwise it opens an email with a written subject and body (`mailto:` with the visitor's choices
   498→   498→filled in), or, for a shop, it points at `#checkout` and the report says it must be wired before
   499→   499→launch. Never fake a payment form.
   500→   500→
   501→   501→**No Chrome or Node 22.** Run `lint.mjs` (Node 18+), take screenshots with whatever browser tool you
   502→   502→have, and critique those. Say in the report that the inspector could not run.
   503→   503→
   504→   504→**No network.** If `assets.mjs` reaches no source, draw the pictures and say in the report that the
   505→   505→open collections could not be searched.
